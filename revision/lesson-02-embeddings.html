PART 1 · THE IDEA IN 60 SECONDS

Turn text into numbers so that similar meaning means nearby numbers

A computer can't compare the meaning of two sentences, only numbers. An embedding model turns any text into a fixed-length list of numbers (a vector), trained so that texts with similar meaning get vectors pointing in similar directions. Then "find text about this" becomes "find the nearest vectors", which a database can do fast.

Showing 40 of the 3072 numbers, as colours (example values). Notice the first two look alike and the third doesn't: similar meaning, similar pattern, even with no words in common.

Analogy: coordinates for meaning. A city has a latitude and longitude, and nearby cities have nearby coordinates. An embedding is a coordinate for a piece of text, on a map with 3072 directions instead of 2. "Who built the lighthouse?" and "Who constructed the tower?" end up neighbours; "fresh fish at the market" lives across town.
3072

numbers per text from gemini-embedding-001, whether you send one word or a whole page.

No single meaning

Number #42 isn't "lighthouse-ness". Only the whole vector, compared with others, means anything.

One way

You can't turn the numbers back into the sentence. It's a fingerprint, not a compression.

Same model, both sides

Chunks and questions must be embedded by the same model, or the numbers aren't comparable.

    PART 3 · COSINE SIMILARITY BY HAND

    Similarity is the angle between two arrows

    Drag the tips of arrow A and arrow B. Cosine similarity only looks at the angle between them: 1 = same direction, 0 = at right angles (unrelated), −1 = opposite. pgvector's <=> returns cosine distance = 1 − similarity, so there smaller is closer.

(Gemini's vectors already come with length 1, so for them cosine and dot product rank the same way.)
    

    



    
  
    PART 4 · HOW IT LEARNS MEANING
    
  
    Pull matching texts together, push unrelated ones apart
    
  
    
    
    Nobody writes rules saying "lighthouse is near tower". The model learns it from huge numbers of pairs that belong together: a question and its answer, a title and its article, two ways of saying the same thing. Training pulls each pair's vectors closer and pushes random unrelated texts away. Repeat millions of times and topics form neighbourhoods. This is called contrastive learning.

    "compare the two pdfs" landed nearest to "quiz me about the pdf", with a clear margin. Similar words, different intent. That's why a vector "quiz" always gets a second opinion from the LLM.

    PART 6 · USES AND GOTCHAS
    
  
    What embeddings are good for, and where they fail
    
  
    
    
    Semantic search
    Find passages by meaning, not exact words. The R in RAG. DocMind: chunk search.
    
    
    Routing and classification
    Compare a message with example phrases per category; nearest category wins. DocMind: the intent router.
    
    
    Recommendations
    "Users who read this also liked…": nearest articles or products.
    
    
    Duplicate detection
    Two support tickets with near-identical vectors are probably the same issue.
    
    
    Clustering
    Group thousands of feedback messages into themes without reading them all.
    
    
    Caching
    Reuse work for a repeated question (exact repeats are safe; "similar" is risky).
    
  
    
  
    Where embeddings go wrong
    
  
    
    
    
      
    ProblemExampleWhat to do
    
      
        
    Shared words, different intent"compare the two pdfs" ≈ "quiz me about the pdf"Second opinion (an LLM) on close or risky calls
    
        
    Negation"flights that are not delayed" ≈ "delayed flights"Filters in code, or let an LLM read the results
    
        
    Exact codes and IDs"error E1042" ≈ "error E1043"Keyword search alongside (hybrid search)
    
        
    Vague follow-ups"and who founded it?"Rewrite into a full question before embedding
    
        
    Mixed modelschunks from model A, question from model BOne model everywhere; re-embed everything when switching
    
        
    Too much text in one vectora 20-page chunk becomes a blurry averageChunk into short passages (lesson 4)
    
          
    
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Storage and cost, quick maths
    
    
    Each number is stored as 4 bytes, so one 3072-number vector ≈ 12 KB. A million chunks ≈ 12 GB of vectors alone. Embedding calls are cheap and fast compared with generating text, but they still count against rate limits (100 per minute on your free tier).
    
    
    bonus Smaller vectors (768 or 1536 numbers) take less space and search faster, with a small quality loss; Gemini's embedding model can return shorter vectors if you ask. pgvector's fast indexes support at most 2,000 dimensions for the normal vector type, so a vector(3072) column would need halfvec or shorter vectors before an index can be added.

    PART 8 · INTERVIEW DRILL
    
  
    25 questions you could get. Answer out loud first, then open.
    
  
    
    
    Say it in 60 seconds: "What are embeddings, and how did you use them?"
    
    
    "An embedding is a list of numbers that represents the meaning of a piece of text. A trained embedding model produces it, and texts with similar meaning get vectors pointing in similar directions, so you can measure similarity with cosine similarity. In my project, DocMind, I split every uploaded PDF into chunks, embed each chunk with Gemini's embedding model into 3072 numbers, and store them in Postgres with pgvector. When a user asks something, I embed the question with the same model and fetch the closest chunks for the LLM to answer from. I also reuse the same embedding to route messages, by comparing it with example phrases for each intent. One thing I learned measuring it: scores are relative. My unrelated test pair still scored 0.59."
    
  
    
  
    Each answer opens with Say this first: the 10-second version to lead with. bonus questions go beyond most intern interviews.
