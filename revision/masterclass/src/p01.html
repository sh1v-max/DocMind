1.0How the first five minutes work

The first block of a 90-minute interview (0–5 min) looks like small talk. It isn’t. The interviewer is deciding three things: can you communicate clearly, are you honest about where you are, and what should I dig into for the next 85 minutes. Whatever you mention in your introduction becomes the map for the rest of the interview. Mention DocMind’s agent loop and you will be asked about it; mention “I know Kubernetes” and you will be asked about that too.

What the interviewer is evaluating in this part (from the bank): honesty, communication, ownership, consistency, motivation, and the ability to explain the employment gap without making excuses.
A self-introduction is a short spoken summary that answers “who are you, what can you do, and why are you here”. Think of it as the README of you: what it is, what it does, how to run it, where it’s going. Without a planned introduction, people either recite their CV in date order (boring, and it buries the strongest part at the end) or ramble. A structured one lets you choose what the interviewer asks next.

Use four beats, about 20 seconds each:

  1. Present: what you are now. “I’m a backend-focused developer building AI applications in Node.js.”
  2. Past (short): how you got here. Degree, then two years of structured self-learning with shipped projects.
  3. Proof: one project in some depth, with one technical decision and one number.
  4. Future: why this role, connected to what you just proved.
 A good trailer shows the best scene and stops. It doesn’t play the whole film from minute one. Every interview, every recruiter call, every networking message. The 30-second version opens LinkedIn DMs; the 90-second version opens interviews.

A version built from your real facts (edit the words, keep the shape):

Sample · about 90 seconds
“I’m Shiv. I’m a backend developer working mostly in Node.js and Express, and for the last few months I’ve focused on AI backends. I finished my B.Tech in computer science from LPU in 2024. Since then I’ve been learning full time in a structured way: I’ve shipped several full-stack projects, kept a GitHub streak of over 300 days, and practised DSA for over 100 days.

Answers stream over server-sent events.

I’m applying for this role because that’s exactly the work I enjoy: making LLM features reliable, with queues, validation, and proper user isolation around them. I’d like to do it with a team that reviews my code.”

Notice what it does: it gives the interviewer five things to pick from (BullMQ, pgvector, router, agent loop, user isolation), and you can defend every one of them.

How the interviewer uses it:

  1. They hear “agent loop I wrote myself” → likely question: “Why not use a framework?”
  2. They hear “scoped to the user from the JWT” → “What happens if I change the document id in the request?”
  3. They hear “about two seconds” → they now trust you know your numbers.
  • Starting with school marks and hobbies.
  • Listing 15 technologies. Each one is an invitation to be quizzed.
  • Memorised delivery that sounds read. Learn the four beats, not the sentences.
  • Claiming “production experience” for personal projects. Say “deployed and live”, which is true and still strong.
 A gap answer explains what you did between graduation and now, why, and what evidence shows it was productive. A two-year gap makes interviewers wonder: was this person stuck, unmotivated, or unable to pass interviews? Silence or vagueness confirms the worry. A specific, honest answer replaces it with evidence.

Formula: fact → choice → evidence → what changed → now.

  • Fact: “I graduated in 2024 and I haven’t had a full-time developer job yet.” Say it plainly; don’t make them dig.
  • Choice: what you decided to do with the time.
  • Evidence: things they can check: GitHub history, live projects, commit messages, write-ups.
  • What changed: what you can do now that you couldn’t then.
  • Now: why you want a team, not more solo work.
 A gap in a CV is like a long gap in a Git history. A messy history with real commits is fine; an empty one raises questions. Show the commits.
Strong answer
“I graduated in 2024. I applied to some roles then but my skills weren’t strong enough yet, so I decided to learn properly instead of taking anything. I set a structure: build real projects end to end, deploy them, and keep a daily habit. My GitHub has a 300-plus day streak, and the projects are live. This year I moved into AI backends with DocMind, where I built the queue, the vector search, auth and an agent loop step by step and wrote down what broke. What I’m missing is working in a team, with code review and real users, and that’s why I’m applying now.”
Weak answers
“The market was bad.” (blames the world) · “I was preparing for government exams and also learning” (unclear focus, unless true, then say it plainly) · “I was freelancing” (if you can’t name a client, don’t say it) · a long story that never reaches “now”.
  1. Interviewer: “What have you been doing since 2024?” → you give fact + choice in two sentences.
  2. They probe: “Why not a job?” → ownership answer (skills weren’t ready), no blame.
  3. They test evidence: “Show me” → you name the live DocMind link and the GitHub history.
  4. They check now: “Why now?” → you want code review, a team and real users.
  5. The topic closes in about 90 seconds, and the next question is about your project, which is where you want it.
  • Apologising repeatedly. One honest sentence is enough.
  • Overclaiming: calling side projects “industry experience”.
  • Inventing freelance work. It falls apart on the first follow-up.
 Every claim you make about yourself should come with a piece of evidence: a project, a decision, a bug, a number. “I’m a fast learner” is said by everyone and believed by no one. “I learned MySQL and Rails in one day for a walk-in interview and could answer their joins questions” is believed.

  2. Interviewer follows the evidence with a technical question (“How did you measure it?”).
  3. You answer from memory with specifics (RateLimit-Policy header, sha256 of your IP, hops 1/2/3).

A: …

Strong

Q: What have you been doing since 2024?

A: Learning full time with a structure. I shipped a few full-stack apps, then this year built DocMind, an AI document chat backend.

Q: What’s in it?

A: Postgres with pgvector, a BullMQ queue for embedding, JWT auth with per-user scoping, and an agent loop with two tools. It’s live.

Q: Why pgvector and not a vector database?

A: I already needed Postgres for users and chats, so vectors in the same database meant one system and joins for ownership. At my scale that’s plenty; at many millions of vectors I’d look at an index or a dedicated store.

Specific, honest, every claim survives a follow-up.

1.4Handling uncomfortable questions

  • “Your CGPA is low.” Agree briefly, don’t argue: “It’s 7.01. My projects are a better measure of what I can do now. Happy to go through one.”
  • “This looks like a tutorial project.” Show the parts no tutorial has: “The idempotent job, the guest-to-user upgrade that keeps files, the measured trust-proxy setting.”
  • “Did AI write this code?” “I used AI assistants, like most developers. I reviewed every change and I can explain any line. Pick a file.” Then be able to.
  • A question you can’t answer. “I don’t know that one. Here’s how I’d reason about it…” Guessing confidently is worse than saying you don’t know.
